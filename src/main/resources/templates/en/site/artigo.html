Provisional abstract

For a mediumship study to show anything, it must show that the information given by the medium did not reach them through ordinary channels: inadvertent cues, cold reading, prior research, the goodwill of whoever rates the reading, or fraud. The literature on anomalous information reception (AIR) already describes protocols that close these doors. Running them, however, depends on a research team and on manual procedures that readers of the published paper cannot check, and scoring remains subject to the judgement of whoever decides whether a statement fits the deceased person.

This paper presents Crivo, an open-source platform that runs an AIR protocol from start to finish with no member of the research team in the loop. The sitter, who is the bereaved person, and the medium answer the same closed questionnaire about the deceased, of whom the medium knows only the first name. The system compares the answers field by field, against the target and against a decoy, another deceased person with a similar profile who serves as a control. Nobody has to judge whether a statement fits. Methodological requirements become properties of the software: what each person can see is restricted in the data layer, not on the screen; answers are cryptographically sealed before any information reaches the medium; the random draw is committed before data collection and revealed at the end; the study plan is preregistered and frozen before any data exist; blinding is lifted automatically when the declared sample is complete; and every step enters a hash-chained audit trail, attested by an independent time-stamping authority.

Null and positive results produce the same report and the same verification package, which anyone can check from the file itself. The paper describes the requirements, derived in part from sceptical critiques, the protocol and its power analysis, the architecture, what the system guarantees and what it cannot guarantee, and the ethical and data protection issues.

Keywords: mediumship; anomalous information reception; blinded protocol; preregistration; reproducibility; audit trail; open-source software.

Resumo

Para que um estudo de mediunidade diga alguma coisa, é preciso mostrar que a informação dada pelo médium não chegou a ele por vias comuns: pistas involuntárias, leitura fria, pesquisa prévia, a boa vontade de quem avalia ou fraude. A literatura de recepção anômala de informação (AIR, de anomalous information reception) já descreve protocolos capazes de fechar essas portas. Executá-los, porém, depende de uma equipe e de regras manuais que quem lê o artigo publicado não tem como conferir, e a avaliação continua sujeita ao julgamento de quem decide se uma afirmação se ajusta à pessoa falecida.

Este artigo apresenta o Crivo, uma plataforma de código aberto que executa um protocolo de AIR do início ao fim sem ninguém da equipe no circuito. O consulente, que é a pessoa enlutada, e o médium respondem ao mesmo questionário fechado sobre o falecido, de quem o médium conhece apenas o primeiro nome. O sistema confere as respostas campo a campo, contra o alvo e contra um chamariz, outra pessoa falecida de perfil semelhante que serve de controle. Ninguém precisa julgar se uma afirmação se ajusta. Os requisitos de método tornam-se propriedades do software: o que cada pessoa pode ver é limitado na camada de dados, e não na tela; as respostas são lacradas criptograficamente antes de qualquer informação chegar ao médium; o sorteio é lacrado antes da coleta e aberto no fim; o plano do estudo é registrado e congelado antes dos dados; o cegamento se desfaz sozinho quando a amostra declarada se completa; e cada passo entra numa trilha de auditoria encadeada, atestada por uma autoridade independente de carimbo do tempo.

Resultados nulos e positivos produzem o mesmo relatório e o mesmo pacote de verificação, que qualquer pessoa confere a partir do próprio arquivo. O artigo descreve os requisitos, derivados também da crítica cética, o protocolo e o cálculo de poder, a arquitetura, o que o sistema garante e o que não pode garantir, e as questões de ética e de proteção de dados.

Palavras-chave: mediunidade; recepção anômala de informação; protocolo cego; pré-registro; reprodutibilidade; auditoria; software livre.

1. Introduction

People who consult a medium after losing someone sometimes leave convinced that they heard things nobody there could have known. Science does not have to confirm or deny that impression in order to take it seriously; it has to ask where the information came from. The question, therefore, is not whether there are reports of mediums who get things right — they are abundant — but whether, under conditions that rule out the conventional explanations, the information produced exceeds what would be expected by chance.

Those explanations are well known and nothing exotic. A person sitting in front of the medium reacts, and the reactions guide what comes next. Vague statements sound exact to someone who wants to recognize them. The medium may have done research beforehand, or simply cheated. And whoever rates what was said tends to keep the hits and forget the misses. A study says something only if each of these doors is closed — and only if it is possible to show, afterwards, that it was.

The experimental literature calls the phenomenon in question anomalous information reception, or AIR: the report of accurate and specific information about a deceased person, without prior knowledge, without sensory feedback and without cheating (Beischel et al., 2015). The term is deliberately neutral as to origin. It does not claim that the information comes from the deceased person, nor from some capacity of the living, such as telepathy (Beischel and Schwartz, 2007). The field's vocabulary follows the same caution: what the medium produces about a deceased person is called a reading, not a message or a communication, words that presuppose where the information comes from. Throughout the text, the sitter is the bereaved person who knew the deceased and whose knowledge about them serves as the reference for checking the readings — the consulente of the Portuguese version of this platform. This paper adopts that neutrality: it describes a method for checking whether the information exists, not a theory about where it would come from.

The empirical picture is divided. Studies with blinding at several layers report above-chance performance (Beischel and Schwartz, 2007; Beischel et al., 2015); others find no effect at all (O'Keeffe and Wiseman, 2005). A recent comparative analysis attributes part of the divergence to details of the design, such as the selection of the mediums and the kind of interaction allowed during the reading (Silva and Moreira-Almeida, 2022).

This paper argues that the bottleneck in the field lies less in theory than in verifiable execution. A blinded study depends on compliance with dozens of small rules — who may know what, and from when on — and that compliance is usually recorded, when it is recorded at all, in manual procedures that the reader of the published paper has no way to audit. The authors of one of the main series of positive studies also point out that all of their data come from a single laboratory (Beischel et al., 2015), which makes independent replication especially valuable.

The proposal is to treat those rules as properties of a software system, implemented and tested with the rigor with which security requirements are tested, and to remove subjective judgement from the scoring. The paper makes four contributions: it organizes the methodological requirements as a threat model (section 3); it describes a paired, closed and fully automated protocol (section 4); it presents the architecture that enforces it (section 5); and it delimits what that architecture guarantees and what it does not (section 6). The following sections deal with ethics, the current state of the project and the reach of what it will be able to show. Being a design paper, the text brings no results: no study with real participants has been conducted.

2. Related work

2.1 Blinded AIR protocols

In research, to blind someone is to prevent that person from knowing something that could, even unintentionally, influence the result; undoing the blinding at the end of the study, revealing what was hidden, is the unblinding. The reference protocol for this work, that of Beischel and Schwartz (2007), blinded three roles at the same time. The medium, who did the reading by telephone, received only the first name of the deceased person and, besides speaking freely, answered questions about their appearance, personality, hobbies and cause of death. The sitters did not take part: they were represented by an experimenter — the proxy sitter — who also knew nothing about them or about the deceased. The deceased were paired two by two, of the same gender, and the same medium read both members of each pair. At the end, each sitter scored, without knowing which was which, the reading made for their own deceased person, called the target, and the reading for the other deceased person of the pair, which served as a control and is called the decoy; then they chose the one that fitted better.

The replication and extension of Beischel et al. (2015), with 20 mediums certified by the group itself after screening and testing, kept that structure and again reported target readings rated better than the decoys. The authors point to the fact that all the data come from a single laboratory as the main limitation. For the description of the laboratory techniques, we use Beischel (2007), without taking it as an assessment of the evidence.

2.2 Methodological criticism and null results

Not every blinded study finds an effect. O'Keeffe and Wiseman (2005) designed a method of their own, with no contact between mediums and sitters and with each sitter rating, statement by statement, every reading in the study, and obtained a null result. The study was criticized for the lack of screening of the mediums and for the format of the items, which combined several statements into one. In Kelly and Arcangel (2011), the first stage had no significant result. The second did, but it changed three things, among them sending the mediums photographs of the deceased — and a photograph says a great deal, from age to the period in which the person lived, which leaves the blinding of that stage incomplete. Cases like these show that apparently small operational details decide the validity of a study.

The objections that weigh most on the design of Crivo, however, come from the sceptical critique (Hyman, 1977, 2003; Wiseman and O'Keeffe, 2001). Cold reading is the technique, deliberate or not, of producing statements that sound specific but fit almost everybody, correcting course from the reactions of the listener (Hyman, 1977). Subjective validation is the other side of the coin: whoever rates the reading finds meaning in what they hear and recognizes a vague statement as exact. To these are added biased scoring and the absence of a stopping rule fixed before data collection, which allows a study to be ended at the moment when the result looks favorable (Hyman, 2003).

2.3 Reviews, meta-analyses and the Brazilian context

A meta-analysis statistically combines the results of several studies to estimate a common effect. The one by Sarraf, Woodley of Menie and Tressoldi (2021) brought together 18 experiments, published in 14 papers between 2001 and 2019, and estimated an effect of 0.18 above chance level, on a standardized proportion index (95% confidence interval: 0.12 to 0.25). The estimate withstood two tests for publication bias, the tendency for positive results to reach the journals more easily than null ones. Milton and Wiseman (1999) recall, however, that a meta-analysis does not replace independent replication: the result depends on which studies go in and on how the differences between them are handled. The lesson came from another literature, that of the ganzfeld, a telepathy experiment under mild sensory deprivation.

Silva and Moreira-Almeida (2022) compared eight recent studies with at least triple blinding, five with a significant result and three without, looking for what distinguished them. Seven conditions coincided with a positive result: selecting mediums with prior evidence; recruiting motivated sitters bereaved by a significant loss; allowing some interaction with the medium, even if through a proxy sitter; giving the medium some information about the deceased; combining free speech with objective questions; scoring both the whole reading and the items; and avoiding a large number of readings and of items. One of the findings is a point that this paper has to face: in the two studies in which the medium interacted with nobody, one of them that of O'Keeffe and Wiseman (2005), the result was null. The authors themselves ask for caution, because the comparison is heuristic and some of the studies do not describe important variables.

In Brazil, Rocha et al. (2014) examined 13 letters psychographed by Chico Xavier (cartas psicografadas), that is, written by the medium and attributed by him to a single deceased person. They identified 99 verifiable items of information and checked them against documents and against the testimony of the deceased person's sister and friends: 98% were classified as a clear and precise fit. The work shows the value of statements that are verifiable item by item. And, precisely because it is a retrospective case study, which examines material produced with no control of leakage whatsoever, it also shows what a prospective and blinded design would add.

2.4 Preregistration and analytical flexibility

A study does not go wrong only through leakage. Choices not declared in data collection and analysis — stopping collection when the result looks good, deciding after the data which measure to report, trying several analyses and showing the one that worked — are enough to make a non-existent effect look statistically significant, that is, unlikely to have arisen by chance (Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn, 2011). Preregistration answers that: publishing, before data collection, the hypotheses, the sample size and the analysis that will be run. It is the scientific equivalent of announcing the bet before the roulette wheel spins, and it makes verifiable the distinction between confirmatory research, which tests what was declared, and exploratory research, which looks for patterns in data already seen (Nosek et al., 2018).

2.5 The gap

To delimit the gap, we carried out an exploratory survey of the software used in studies of mediumship and of psi, the generic term in parapsychology for extrasensory perception and related phenomena. The search was made in November 2026, on the open web and in code repositories, with terms in English and in Portuguese that combined the domain (mediumship, AIR, parapsychology, psi, ganzfeld, extrasensory perception) with the function sought (software, platform, open source, blinding, randomization, preregistration, audit trail, automatic scoring). What turned up falls into five families (Table 1).

The closest neighbor is the Transparent Psi Project (Kekecs et al., 2023). It starts from the same thesis as this paper — that the credibility of a result should be verifiable by third parties, and not presupposed — and applies it to a multicenter replication of a precognition experiment. Crivo should be read as an extension of that line to a domain in which there are several human roles to keep blind from one another, and not as an idea without precedent.

There is also a precedent for the idea of proving the date of a protocol by recording its cryptographic fingerprint, or hash, with the date attested by third parties — the timestamp, explained in section 5. In clinical research, Irving and Holden (2016) proposed anchoring that record in a blockchain, a public and distributed record like that of Bitcoin; the paper was retracted by the authors themselves in 2017, after open peer review pointed out methodological problems. That recommends caution in citing it and reinforces the choice, here, of an established standard, RFC 3161, which is the internet standard for timestamps (Adams et al., 2001). Finally, there is an open-source project on SourceForge for online psi experiments, not updated since 2013; we found in it no preregistration, no blinding by role and no audit trail.

The gap, in the form in which the paper can state it: in the sources consulted, we did not find an open platform that runs a mediumship protocol end to end and that treats the blinding between roles, the randomization, the preregistration and the scoring as properties verifiable by third parties from published artifacts. The pieces exist, but separately: in other domains or as a manual procedure.

3. Requirements: a threat model

In security engineering, a threat model lists everything that can go wrong in a system and associates with each risk the corresponding defense. Here, each conventional explanation for a hit is treated as a threat: if it is capable of producing the result, the result proves nothing. Table 2 relates each threat to the source that describes it and to the control that Crivo sets against it; where the control is only partial, the table says so. Several controls use cryptographic devices, such as hashes, commitments and timestamps, explained in simple terms at the beginning of section 5.

Most of these controls do not depend on anyone remembering to apply them: they are in the design of the database and in the tests, as section 5 shows. The last row, however, has no complete solution. With sign-up open, a medium and a sitter who know each other can arrange their answers using accounts that nothing links together; the system reduces that risk, but does not eliminate it, and section 6 records it among the limits.

4. The protocol: automated structured reading

4.1 Core idea

The protocol starts from a simple idea: the sitter and the medium answer the same closed questionnaire about the deceased person. The sitter answers because they knew the person; the medium, knowing only the first name. Since there is no free text, there is nothing to interpret. The checking is a comparison of equality between options, done by the system, and nobody — not even the sitter — decides whether a statement “fits”. The idea of asking the medium about specific aspects of the deceased person's life was already in Beischel and Schwartz (2007); what changes is that the answers are closed into options.

The trade-off has a cost, and the protocol declares it. Objective scoring and full automation are gained; the proxy sitter and free response are lost, precisely two of the conditions that Silva and Moreira-Almeida (2022) associated with a positive result. For that reason, a result from this protocol, positive or null, does not count as a replication of the studies with a proxy sitter in the literature.

The questionnaire in force, version v2-rascunho, has 23 fields. Three of them — gender, age range at death and period of death — serve to form the pairs and stay out of the score: they are equal within each pair, by construction, and gender is usually given away by the name itself. The other 20 enter the score and fall into six themes: the circumstances of the death (how it happened, the cause and the place); the family (marital status, number of children, position among siblings and the kinship of the person who made the request); the life course (education, occupation and the kind of place where the person lived); personality (temperament and most striking trait); appearance (height, build, hair and glasses); and beliefs and habits (religion, pastime, smoking and pet). Each field has from two to ten options, plus “I don't know”, for the sitter, and “Nothing came”, for the medium, who answers only the scored fields. The complete questionnaire, with all the options, is published on the protocol page.

The fields were not chosen only for what seems relevant. In a closed questionnaire, the signal a field can yield depends on how far it distinguishes one deceased person from another in the same stratum: in a field in which almost everybody gives the same answer, target and decoy coincide almost always, and not even a perfect reading stands out (section 4.6 gives the formula). Hence the criteria. Each field asks for a single fact that the sitter can answer; the options are mutually exclusive and cover all cases; none of them dominates; nothing in the score can be deduced from the first name or from the stratum, such as the region or the generation; no field identifies the person on its own, because the answers go into the public verification package; the themes are the ones the literature asks the medium about, such as appearance, personality, hobbies and cause of death (Beischel et al., 2015); and no option forces anyone to name a violent death, which fits under “another external cause”. The previous version, v1-rascunho, had 13 scored fields, some with an almost universal option, such as “had children”. The revision halves the number of pairs needed for the same effect (section 4.6). It is still a draft: it lacks review by researchers in the field and cognitive interviewing with sitters, which checks whether each question is understood as intended and how much time filling it in takes.

4.2 Flow

From preregistration to feedback, a study goes through eight stages. Apart from the preregistration, done by the researcher, and the participants' answers, everything is done by the system.

  1. Preregistration. The researcher declares and freezes the hypotheses, the number of pairs (N), the questionnaire, the pairing criterion, the medium's deadline, the inclusion criteria, the analysis and the exclusion rules. The commitment to the randomization seed goes in the same document (section 5.4).
  2. Request. The sitter creates the account, confirms the e-mail address and makes the request: the first name of the deceased person and the answers to the questionnaire. The answers are locked, and the audit trail receives the corresponding commitment (section 5.3). The request may precede the freeze, since nobody sees the answers before the end; only a request made in the questionnaire version that the preregistration declares, however, enters the study.
  3. Pairing and randomization. The system forms pairs of locked requests from the same stratum, that is, with the same gender, the same age range at death and the same period of death, and draws a medium for each pair.
  4. Dispatch. The medium receives the two first names of the pair, in a randomized order, and the blank questionnaire. The medium does not know who the sitters are and has no contact with them.
  5. Reading. For each name, the medium answers every field or marks “Nothing came”. On submission, the reading is locked, also with a commitment in the trail. If the declared deadline expires, the pair goes to another medium, drawn from the same seed.
  6. Unblinding. When the N pairs have both readings, the system itself closes data collection and undoes the blinding: it shows which medium read each pair and opens the commitments, disclosing the secrets that kept them sealed — the salts and the seed (sections 5.3 and 5.4).
  7. Scoring and analysis. The system checks the commitments, scores the readings, runs the preregistered analysis and publishes the report and the verification package.
  8. Feedback. The sitter sees the reading made for their own deceased person next to their own answers; the medium sees the scores of their own readings.

4.3 Visibility matrix

The blinding is specified as a matrix: in the rows, the study's information; in the columns, the roles; in each cell, what that role may see and from when on (Table 3). Each forbidden cell corresponds to an automated test that tries to violate it against a real database. Note that the administrator — the researcher who runs the study — knows who takes part, but is blind to the answers, the readings and the link between requests and mediums until the unblinding.

4.4 Automatic scoring

The scoring rule fits in a few lines. In a pair formed by the deceased persons A and B, we call RA the reading made with A's name and RB the one made with B's name; for simplicity, A and B also designate the sitters of each deceased person. Only the comparable fields count: the scored fields that both sitters answered, without “I don't know”. The set is the same for the target and for the decoy, so that neither of the two has a greater chance of a hit. For a reading R and a sitter X, s(R, X) is the number of comparable fields in which the reading's answer coincides with X's; “Nothing came” never coincides. The pair's difference is

d = [s(RA, A) − s(RA, B)] + [s(RB, B) − s(RB, A)]

Each bracket asks whether a reading looks more like the person for whom it was made than like the other person of the pair. If the readings carry no information about the targets, the two comparisons tend to balance out, and d stays around zero.

An example, with fictitious names

Suppose a pair of men who died between the ages of 60 and 79, between one and five years ago: Antônio, Ana's father, and Carlos, Beatriz's husband. Ana and Beatriz answered the 20 scored fields, with no “I don't know” at all, and all are comparable. The medium drawn receives the two names and fills in the questionnaire twice, once for each name. The reading made for Antônio coincides with Ana's answers in 7 fields and with Beatriz's in 5; the one made for Carlos coincides with Beatriz's in 5 and with Ana's, also in 5. The pair's difference is d = (7 − 5) + (5 − 5) = +2.

On its own, that number means nothing: one pair may give +2 by chance, just as another may give −2. The analysis asks whether, summed over all the pairs in the study, the positive values predominate beyond what chance would explain.

The decoy is indispensable because the number of hits, on its own, says nothing. Frequent answers — “died of a heart condition”, “was married”, “had children” — coincide with those of a great many people. By comparing the same reading with the target and with a decoy of a similar profile, the frequency of each answer cancels out, and what is left is how far the reading fits the target more than the decoy. That is why cold reading, which lives off frequent statements, earns no points in this protocol. And, since the same medium does both readings of the pair, that medium's tendency to repeat certain answers enters on both sides of the difference and also cancels out.

This makes it possible to declare the chance level before data collection, instead of estimating it from the data. The null hypothesis, the one the study puts to the test, is that the reading made with A's name carries no information about A that it does not also carry about B. If it is true, swapping the two sitters' answers with each other merely reverses the sign of d. Hence d is symmetric around zero, and, among the pairs with d different from zero, the chance of d being positive is 50%. Since the first name may carry some information about the person, that hypothesis does not come for free; section 6 discusses the leakage that remains.

4.5 Analysis

The unit of analysis is the pair: each pair contributes a single d. The primary analysis is the one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the d values of all the pairs. In simple terms, the test ranks the differences by size and checks whether the positive ones weigh more than the negative ones beyond what chance would allow; it is one-sided because the hypothesis predicts only positive differences. With up to 20 pairs in which d is different from zero, the test is exact; above that, it uses the normal approximation, a formula that becomes accurate with larger samples, and the report says which of the two was used.

Two secondary analyses look at the same data from other angles. The exact binomial sign test ignores the size of d and asks only whether the positive pairs exceed half. The permutation test shuffles the readings among the targets of the study, within each stratum, and checks how often an assignment made at random produces as many coincidences as the true one. It follows the logic of the Pratt and Birge procedure, probably the most widely adopted in the field, and does not assume that the answers of one and the same reading are independent of one another (O'Keeffe and Wiseman, 2005). The permutation is exact as long as the possible arrangements fit in an enumeration; above that, it uses 100,000 arrangements drawn from the revealed seed, which anyone can redo.

What the analysis does not do is also declared: multiplying the probability of a hit in each field as if they were independent, as Hyslop did in 1919. That procedure inflates the evidence, because answers about one and the same person are correlated (O'Keeffe and Wiseman, 2005).

The stopping rule is binding: data collection ends at the declared N, with no interim analysis. And there is no way to peek, because the scores do not exist before the unblinding — there is no partial result to consult, not even for the researcher. Any analysis that is not in the preregistration appears in the report labeled as exploratory.

4.6 Expected effect and sample size

Statistical power is the chance that a study will detect an effect that does in fact exist. A study with low power tends to come out null even when there is an effect, and a null result of that kind does not distinguish the absence of an effect from the lack of sample. That is why the preregistration declares the expected effect; the platform computes how many pairs that effect requires and writes in the frozen document whether the declared N is enough. It does not refuse a study that is too small, because a pilot is legitimate, but the report repeats what the preregistration said.

The difficulty is that there is no prior estimate for this protocol. The published effects come from free readings scored by sitters. In Beischel et al. (2015), the items of the target readings were judged correct more often than those of the decoys: 52.8% versus 36.6% in the question section, with d = 0.75, and 37.0% versus 24.5% in the free speech, with d = 0.49. In the meta-analysis of Sarraf et al. (2021), the combined effect is 0.18 above chance on the proportion index, which is equivalent to being correct on 68% of choices between two options, where chance gives 50%. Those values of d do not transfer to this protocol: they measure something else, with a different variability. What does transfer is the shape of the effect, by means of a model of the protocol itself.

The model is this. In each field, the medium marks “Nothing came” with probability 10%; in the remaining cases, with probability θ, it gives the true answer of the person whose name it received and, failing that, guesses according to the frequency of the options. θ is the information rate of the reading: θ = 0 is the null hypothesis, and θ = 0.05 means that the reading brings the true answer, beyond what guessing would bring, in one field out of twenty. Each sitter marks “I don't know” with probability 8%. In this model, in a comparable field, the chance that the reading coincides with the target exceeds the chance that it coincides with the decoy by

Δ = 0.9 · θ · (1 − Σ pk2)

in which pk is the frequency of option k and Σ pk2 is the chance that two deceased persons from the same stratum coincide in that field by chance. It is the formula that guides the choice of the fields (section 4.1): with an almost universal option, the parenthesis approaches zero, and the field yields no signal, whatever the reading. The frequencies used are assumptions for a typical stratum, that of people who died between the ages of 60 and 79, and will be replaced by the observed frequencies before the freeze of a real study.

A simulation redoes, over thousands of fictitious studies, the platform's scoring and Wilcoxon test, with the ties and the zeros that an integer d produces. Under the null hypothesis, it rejects 5.0% of the time, as it should. The code is in the repository (paper/poder/SimulacaoPoder.java), runs with the JDK alone and uses a fixed seed, so that anyone obtains the same numbers. The results for version v2-rascunho are in Table 4. The dz column is the standardized effect of the pair, the mean of d divided by its standard deviation.

The table makes it possible to put the two numbers from the literature on the same scale. The meta-analysis's proportion of 68% correct corresponds, here, to the proportion of positive pairs, and falls between θ = 0.06 and θ = 0.08: a dz close to 0.42, which calls for some 40 pairs. The difference of 12 to 16 percentage points per item in Beischel et al. (2015) corresponds to θ between 0.20 and 0.25, an effect that 20 pairs would be enough to detect. There are three reasons to expect less than that in this protocol. Published effects tend to overestimate the true ones, even when they pass the tests for publication bias. The two studies without interaction analyzed by Silva and Moreira-Almeida (2022) came out null, and here there is no interaction at all. And the closed format discards the free and specific statements, which tend to be the most informative. The simulation itself is optimistic on one point: it draws the fields independently, when, in real life, cause of death and mode of death, for example, go together, and each correlated field adds less information.

The proposal, then, is to plan for half the effect of the meta-analysis: θ = 0.04, with 61% positive pairs and dz = 0.25. The platform's formula, which corrects for the efficiency of the Wilcoxon test relative to the t test, calls for 114 pairs for that dz; the simulation, with ties and zeros, arrives at 120. The confirmatory study therefore declares N = 120 pairs, which means 240 sitters and, with the ceiling of 10 pairs per medium, at least 12 mediums. With that N, power is 84% for the planned effect and 99% for the effect equivalent to that of the meta-analysis. For smaller effects, such as θ = 0.02, it falls to 36%: a null result with 120 pairs would make an effect of the planned size or larger unlikely, but would not say anything about smaller effects, and the report has to say so. The revision of the questionnaire weighs in that calculation: with version v1-rascunho, the same θ = 0.04 would require about 250 pairs.

A pilot study of 30 pairs, planned before the confirmatory one (section 8), has power of 38% for the planned effect and serves another purpose: testing the flow, measuring the frequency of “I don't know” and of “Nothing came” and observing the real frequencies of the options, which enter the simulation in place of the assumed ones. The p-value of the pilot decides nothing, and the estimate of θ it produces is too imprecise, on its own, to reduce the size of the confirmatory study. The final choice of the effect and of N is up to whoever runs the study and is made before the data, in the preregistration; what this paper does is declare the proposal and the way to redo it.

5. Architecture

This section describes how the software enforces the protocol. Three cryptographic ideas support almost all the guarantees, and it is worth presenting them first.

In simple terms

A hash is a fingerprint of a piece of content: a short sequence, computed from it, which changes completely if a single comma changes. From the hash there is no way back to the content, except by trying all the possibilities, which is feasible only when they are few (section 5.3). Crivo uses SHA-256, a standard.

A commitment works like a sealed envelope: the hash of a piece of information kept secret is published and, when the secret is revealed, anyone can check that it is the same as before.

A timestamp is the digital equivalent of a notary: an independent authority signs that a certain hash already existed at a given instant.

5.1 Overview

Crivo is a modular monolith, that is, a single program divided into modules whose boundaries are verified by tests. It is written in Java 21, with Spring Boot and Spring Modulith, keeps the data in PostgreSQL, with the schema migrations done by Flyway, and renders the interface on the server with Thymeleaf. The choice meets a project constraint: the system has to be operable by a single maintainer, and operational complexity is itself a source of error. For the same reason, an auditor runs everything on their own machine with the Java development kit (JDK) alone, without a container: in development, the application starts an embedded instance of PostgreSQL by itself.

5.2 Blinding as a data invariant

In computing, an invariant is a condition that has to hold in any state of the system. In Crivo, the blinding is one of them: it is not applied on the screen, but in the data layer. Each role has its own queries and objects which, by construction, do not contain the fields forbidden to it: the field is not hidden in the interface, it simply does not reach the server's response. The data that identify people are kept encrypted, that is, enciphered, in a separate schema of the database, which a single module may read; the rest of the system works with pseudonymous codes, which do not reveal who the person is. And the rules that cannot fail are enforced by the database itself, not only by the application code: the role of an account, sitter or medium, does not change; no name is released to the medium without a locked request; and no score may exist before the unblinding.

5.3 Salted commitments

A commitment made with the plain hash of the answers would be fragile. Since each field has few options, the number of possible combinations is small enough for a computer to test them one by one until it finds the one that reproduces the published hash — and the audit trail is public. That is why the commitment mixes into the answers a salt, a random 32-byte number, kept encrypted and revealed only at the unblinding. Without the salt, testing the combinations leads nowhere. The rule holds both for the sitter's request and for the medium's reading, and the way it is computed is public, so that a third party can reimplement it.

5.4 Randomization with commitment and reveal

All the draws in the study — the medium of each pair and the order in which the two names are presented to them — derive from a seed, a secret 32-byte number produced by a cryptographic generator and kept encrypted. Each draw is a public function of the seed and of recorded inputs; the list of eligible mediums, for example, changes over the course of the study, and for that reason it is recorded at each draw, with the hash in the trail. Since the pairs are formed little by little, there is no list of draws to commit to in advance: the commitment published in the preregistration is to the seed itself. Once the seed is revealed at the end, anyone can redo all the draws and check that none was hand-picked.

5.5 Frozen preregistration

The preregistration is a canonical document, written in a single form so that the same content always produces the same hash. It is digested by SHA-256 and carries the whole questionnaire as an annex. Once frozen, the database refuses to alter it: amendments create a new version, dated and justified, and the original remains visible.

5.6 Audit trail and external anchoring

Every event of the study enters an append-only table: records are added to it, but none can be altered, and each one carries the hash of the previous one. It works like a minute book in which each page carries the fingerprint of the previous page: altering an old record forces all the ones that came afterwards to be redone. And this is not a matter of convention: the database itself refuses to alter or delete records of that table.

The chain alone does not prevent whoever operates the installation from rewriting it entirely, recomputing all the hashes. That is why the head of the trail — the hash of the last event, which summarizes all the previous ones — is timestamped by an independent time-stamping authority, under RFC 3161. The timestamp comes right after each milestone of the study, such as the freeze of the preregistration, the reveal of the seed and the publication of the report, and once a day, if the trail has changed. Today the authority is FreeTSA, a free service. Only the hash leaves the server, and the tokens are public, so that anyone can check them with common tools, such as OpenSSL, without depending on Crivo.

5.7 Automatic cycle and unblinding

A periodic cycle on the server, run every minute, forms the pairs, draws the mediums, controls the deadlines, resends expired requests and, when N is reached, unblinds the study. The cycle is idempotent, that is, running it twice in a row changes nothing. Nobody chooses the moment of the unblinding, not even the researcher. Until then, the trail links no medium to any pair: the link is kept in the database and only becomes public afterwards.

5.8 Third-party verification

Verification happens at two levels. Whoever operates the installation, or an auditor with access to it, uses a command-line tool, airlab verify, which redoes the hash chain, checks the hash of each preregistration, re-checks the timestamps and, once the seed has been revealed, reproduces the committed draw. Whoever has access only to what is public has the studies page, which shows the preregistration and counts during data collection and the whole report after the unblinding, and the verification package.

The package is written once only, at the unblinding, and its hash enters the trail, which is then timestamped. It brings the frozen preregistration, the revealed seed, the answers of each request and of each reading used in the scoring, the salt of each reading, the dispatches to the mediums with the list of eligible mediums for each draw, the scores per pair, the three analyses, the report and the study's trail. A second tool, airlab verificar-pacote, checks all of that from the file, with no database and no server: it redoes the chain, checks the preregistration and the seed, redoes each draw and each reading commitment, recomputes the score of each pair and the three analyses and checks the hash of the report.

For data protection reasons, the package does not bring the first name of the deceased persons or the salt of the requests (section 7). The cost is declared. The commitments of the requests cannot be redone from outside and are attested by the platform, which checks them at the unblinding — and the tool says so, instead of staying silent. The pairing cannot be redone from outside either, because it depends on the flags for linked accounts, which are not public.

6. Guarantees and limits

Software does not make a study valid by itself. This section says, as precisely as possible, what Crivo guarantees and what it does not guarantee. It is worth distinguishing two kinds of guarantee. The guarantees about the software, such as that no role receives forbidden information, come from the open source code and from the tests, which anyone can read and run. The guarantees about one particular study come from the artifacts it leaves behind: the preregistration, the timestamps, the trail and the verification package.

What the system guarantees, verifiably

  • The preregistration and the commitment to the randomization already existed at the instant attested by the timestamp of the freeze.
  • The sitter's answers were locked before the name reached the medium, and the reading was locked before the unblinding. In the case of the reading, anyone can check this with the package. In the case of the request, the order of the events is public and timestamped, but the correspondence between the published answers and the commitment is attested by the platform (section 5.8).
  • No role received, through the system, information forbidden by the visibility matrix; this is what the tests that try to violate each cell support.
  • The recorded sequence of events was not altered after being timestamped.
  • The draws were the ones that the committed seed determines, and the scoring and the analyses are deterministic: anyone can redo them from the package.

What the system does not guarantee

  • It does not distinguish survival from telepathy with the living: the answers exist in the sitter's memory and in the database.
  • It has no proxy sitter. Since the two studies without interaction analyzed by Silva and Moreira-Almeida (2022) came out null, a null result here does not refute the protocols with a proxy.
  • The closed questionnaire captures only what fits in the fields. The free and specific statements, which tend to be the most striking in a reading, stay out of the score.
  • The first name carries information: the gender and, sometimes, the generation and the region. The pairing and the fields outside the score reduce that leakage, without eliminating it, and a positive result has to be read in the light of it.
  • Leakage outside the system, such as a conversation between participants, and collusion between a medium and a sitter who know each other and use accounts with no apparent link are not detectable with certainty. The draw of the medium and the flagging of linked accounts reduce the risk.
  • A rewriting of the trail between a milestone and the next timestamp, in a window of about one minute in normal operation, is not detected, and a single timestamping authority is a single point of trust. The next phase plans to add an anchor that depends on no operator, OpenTimestamps, anchored in Bitcoin.
  • The commitments of the requests and the pairing cannot be redone from outside (section 5.8).
  • Construct validity — whether the questionnaire measures what it is intended to measure — and generalization to other mediums and other conditions are questions of method, not of software.

The delimitation is deliberate: a system that claims more than it can guarantee undermines the credibility it sets out to produce.

7. Ethical considerations and data protection

The studies involve bereaved people and sensitive information: the life and death of someone close and, by extension, the beliefs of those who take part. Merely signing up as a medium may reveal religious conviction, which the Brazilian data protection law (LGPD) classifies as sensitive personal data, alongside health data (Brasil, 2018, art. 5º, II). The answers about the deceased person, which include the cause of death and religion, receive the same care.

The measures are the usual ones in research with sensitive data, with the difference that the system enforces them: minimization, that is, collecting only what is necessary; pseudonymization; encryption at rest; versioned consent; withdrawal at any moment, with deletion of the identifiable data; and data kept in Brazil. The verification package is public, but it brings no first name and no account data, and participants are informed, before accepting the agreement, that a withdrawal after the unblinding does not alter the package already published.

Sign-up opens only with the approval of a Brazilian research ethics committee (CEP), in the CEP/Conep system, and with a reachable human contact, because someone may sign up in acute grief; the system enforces both conditions. The protocol page says what the protocol is not: it is not a letter or a message from the deceased person, nor an answer to questions from the sitter. The feedback uses careful language and recalls that an individual result says nothing about the deceased person or about the medium, because what the study assesses is the set as a whole. And no artificial intelligence model takes part in the rating or in the scoring.

7.1 Applicable regulations

In Brazil, research with human beings is governed by Lei nº 14.874/2024, which established the National System of Ethics in Research with Human Beings (Sistema Nacional de Ética em Pesquisa com Seres Humanos), and by Decreto nº 12.651/2025, which regulates it (Brasil, 2024; Brasil, 2025). Under the decree's transitional rules, the resolutions of the National Health Council remain in force in whatever does not conflict with the law, until the new national body publishes rules of its own, and protocols continue to be analyzed by the CEP/Conep system. The texts were checked in November 2026, and a real study has to check them again on the date of submission. The following apply to Crivo:

  • Resolução CNS nº 466/2012, with the general guidelines: free and informed consent, assessment of risks and benefits and protection of participants in situations of vulnerability (Conselho Nacional de Saúde, 2012);
  • Resolução CNS nº 510/2016, specific to research in the human and social sciences that uses data obtained directly from participants, which admits forms of recording consent suited to the medium, including the electronic one, and grades the requirements by risk (Conselho Nacional de Saúde, 2016). An online questionnaire, with no intervention, comes close to what that resolution describes; it is up to the committee, and to the field of the proposing institution, to say whether the study follows this resolution or 466;
  • Resolução CNS nº 674/2022, which classifies research and defines the processing of protocols according to risk (Conselho Nacional de Saúde, 2022);
  • Ofício Circular nº 2/2021 of Conep, the circular letter with the guidance for research with any stage in a virtual environment: describing each online stage, with the screens; presenting the consent form before any question and allowing the participant to keep a copy; guaranteeing the right not to answer and to withdraw consent; and declaring the risks specific to the virtual environment and the limits of confidentiality (Comissão Nacional de Ética em Pesquisa, 2021);
  • the LGPD, under which the study's sensitive data — the religious conviction of whoever signs up as a medium, and the religion and the cause of death of the deceased person — may be processed only with specific and prominent consent (Brasil, 2018, art. 11, I). The provision that waives consent for studies carried out by a research body (Brasil, 2018, art. 11, II, c) does not apply as long as the study does not have a research institution as the responsible party.

7.2 What the regulations require and what already exists

Some requirements Crivo already meets by construction. Consent is recorded at the creation of the account, with the version of the agreement accepted. Participants can delete the account and withdraw the request at any moment, without justification, and the identifiable data are deleted. The scored fields have “I don't know”. The questionnaire is public before sign-up, and the risks of the virtual environment have a technical answer: identifiable data encrypted, in a database schema read by a single module, and no personal data in system logs.

Others depend on decisions to be taken with the committee and with the proposing institution:

  • Consent form. The current agreement summarizes the study, but it is not a complete free and informed consent form (termo de consentimento livre e esclarecido, TCLE). The TCLE, in the committee's template, with risks, benefits and the contacts of the researcher and of the CEP, will be shown before the questionnaire, in place of the current agreement, and sent by e-mail to the participant.
  • Right not to answer. The three pairing fields are mandatory, because without them there is no pair. Either the TCLE explains this, or an “I prefer not to answer” option is added, which leaves the request out of the pairing.
  • Vulnerability. The sitter is in mourning, sometimes recent. Besides the human contact and the pointer to the Centro de Valorização da Vida (CVV), Brazil's emotional support helpline, a minimum time since the death for sign-up remains to be decided. Sign-up accepts only people aged 18 or over, by a mandatory declaration, separate from acceptance of the agreement; the platform does not ask for the date of birth, which the research does not use, and does not check the declaration against a document.
  • Re-identification. The package publishes the answers without a name, but someone who knew the deceased person may recognize the set. The fields were chosen so as not to identify anyone on their own (section 4.1); combined with the stratum, however, they may identify someone to a person who already knows the family. The TCLE has to say so. The LGPD protects living people, but the Civil Code allows the family to defend the personality rights of someone who has died (Brasil, 2002, art. 12, parágrafo único), and the answers also speak of the person who made the request, whose kinship is one of the fields.
  • Mediums. They are participants just as much as the sitters, with an agreement of their own: signing up reveals religious conviction, and published performance may affect the reputation of anyone who is identified. In the report, each medium appears only by their pseudonymous code.
  • Custody of the data. The Conep circular letter recommends transferring the collected data to a local device and deleting them from the virtual environment; public verification requires that the trail, the report and the package stay online. The proposal is to delete the identifiable data at the end of the study and keep online only what is pseudonymized and public, with that justification written into the project.
  • Proposing institution. Submission to the CEP/Conep system requires an institution and a responsible researcher affiliated with it. The author of this paper has no affiliation to a research institution, and for that reason a real study depends on a group in the field to run it.

8. Current status and planned evaluation

The whole flow is implemented, from sign-up to the report and the verification package, with blinding and integrity tests that run against a real database at every change to the code. No study with real participants has been conducted: in production, sign-up remains closed until the approval of the ethics committee, and the questionnaire is still under revision. The planned evaluation has three stages, in this order:

  1. Property tests: automated tests that try, systematically, to violate each cell of the visibility matrix and to tamper with the trail.
  2. Full dry run: the whole protocol, with fictitious participants and deliberate attempts to break the blinding.
  3. External audit: an independent researcher receives only the public material and tries to reproduce the analysis and confirm the integrity of the study.

Only after those stages is a pilot study planned.

9. Discussion and conclusion

Crivo trades ecological validity for control. Ecological validity is the similarity between the conditions of the study and those in which the phenomenon usually occurs — here, a real consultation, with conversation and free response. The analysis of Silva and Moreira-Almeida (2022) suggests that this similarity matters, and the protocol gives up a good part of it. The consequences have to be stated clearly. A null result in this protocol will say only that, under these conditions and with this sample size, there was no evidence of above-chance performance; it will not refute mediumship or the protocols with a proxy sitter. A positive result will have in its favor the fact that it was obtained without anyone judging the fit and with every stage checkable by those who doubt it. Even so, it will have to be read in the light of what the first name may reveal, and it will not distinguish between the hypotheses about the origin of the information.

The design also opens paths. Several groups can run the same software and publish in the same verification format, which allows a multicenter replication in which no laboratory has to trust another. Sceptical researchers are natural users of the verification material, and the design was conceived with them in mind. Two extensions are on the horizon: the blind qualification of mediums by the same mechanism, before entry into a study, since the selection of mediums with prior evidence is one of the conditions that the literature associates with a positive result (Sarraf et al., 2021; Silva and Moreira-Almeida, 2022); and free-response protocols with external judges.

In short, Crivo proposes to move the operational details of a mediumship study from the domain of trust to that of verification: each blinding rule becomes a test, each draw becomes a public commitment, and each event becomes a link in a timestamped chain. The aim is not to favor one conclusion, but to allow any conclusion, positive or null, to be checked by those who doubt it.
